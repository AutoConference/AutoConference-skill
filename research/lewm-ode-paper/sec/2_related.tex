\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We situate \method{} along four intersecting lines: JEPA-style predictive
representation learning; LeWM / SIGReg latent world models for control;
continuous-time (Neural ODE) dynamics, including \PriorODEWorld{} as
related prior art; and latent model-based planning with CEM / MPC.
Throughout, we emphasize a deliberately narrow positioning:
\emph{a drop-in ODE predictor on an otherwise unchanged LeWM stack}
(our \method{}), distinct from the full continuous-time world-model
agenda of \PriorODEWorld{}~\cite{liu2026odeworld}.

\subsection{JEPA, I-JEPA, and V-JEPA}
\label{sec:rel-jepa}

Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) were proposed as a
blueprint for predictive world models that operate in representation
space rather than pixel space
\cite{lecun2022path,assran2023jepa}.
Instead of reconstructing the next observation $y$ from a context $x$,
a JEPA encodes both sides and trains a predictor to map the context
embedding toward the target embedding, optionally conditioned on a
latent variable that captures irreducible uncertainty.
The conceptual payoff is abstraction: the target encoder may discard
unpredictable high-frequency detail, yielding representations that are
both informative and predictable---a prerequisite for planning under
partial observability.

I-JEPA~\cite{assran2023jepa} instantiates this idea for images.
From a single large context block, a narrow predictor forecasts the
embeddings of several masked target blocks; targets come from an
EMA-updated target encoder, and the loss is applied only in embedding
space.
By avoiding hand-crafted view augmentations and pixel reconstruction,
I-JEPA learns semantic features that transfer to classification,
counting, and depth.
The masking recipe---large semantic targets plus a spatially
distributed context---is central: too-local targets encourage
low-level texture matching, while an uninformative context collapses
prediction to a prior.

V-JEPA~\cite{bardes2024vjepa} extends feature prediction to video.
Models are trained solely with a masked feature-prediction objective
on large video corpora, without pretrained image encoders, text,
negatives, or reconstruction.
Frozen-backbone evaluation shows strong motion and appearance
transfer (e.g., Kinetics, Something-Something, ImageNet), supporting
the claim that predicting video features is a viable stand-alone
pretraining objective.
V-JEPA~2~\cite{assran2025vjepa2} scales this recipe to web-scale video
and, crucially for control, post-trains an action-conditioned latent
world model (V-JEPA~2-AC) that autoregressively predicts future
embeddings given actions, enabling zero-shot robotic planning with
image goals.
That line demonstrates JEPA latents can support planning, but the
predictor remains discrete / autoregressive in latent space.

\method{} inherits the JEPA loss geometry---predict embeddings, not
pixels---but asks a different question than I-/V-JEPA pretraining:
given a control-oriented JEPA world model already trained
end-to-end from pixels (\LeWM{}), does the \emph{form} of the
predictor (discrete AR vs.\ continuous ODE) matter under matched
data and planning?

\subsection{LeWM, SIGReg, and latent world models for control}
\label{sec:rel-lewm}

Classical world models learn compact dynamics for imagination or
planning from pixels
\cite{ha2018worldmodels,hafner2019planet,hafner2020dreamer}.
Many reconstruct observations (or discrete tokens) and couple the
dynamics to an actor--critic trained in imagination
\cite{hafner2020dreamer,hafner2023dreamerv3}, or to online MPC in
latent space \cite{hansen2022tdmpc}.
JEPA-style control models instead drop the decoder and score
predictions directly in embedding space, reducing the incentive to
waste capacity on pixel-perfect detail that may be irrelevant for
control.

A recurring failure mode for non-contrastive JEPA training is
\emph{representation collapse}: embeddings that ignore inputs yet
trivially satisfy a prediction objective (constant or low-rank
codes).
LeJEPA~\cite{balestriero2025lejepa} argues that the isotropic Gaussian
is an optimal embedding distribution for minimizing downstream
prediction risk, and introduces Sketched Isotropic Gaussian
Regularization (SIGReg) to enforce it.
SIGReg projects embeddings onto random unit directions and applies a
sketched Epps--Pulley characteristic-function statistic per slice,
pushing coordinates toward $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ with linear
time/memory and without stop-gradient teachers, negative pairs, or
BYOL-style EMA heuristics.

\LeWM{}~\cite{maes2026lewm} brings this recipe to end-to-end pixel
world models for control.
Its design is deliberately lean: a ViT encoder trained from scratch,
an action-conditioned predictor, MSE next-embedding loss, and SIGReg
with weight $\lambda{=}0.09$ and $M{=}1024$ projections.
Planning uses CEM in latent space against a goal embedding derived
from a future observation.
Reported PushT success under full-data training is high
($96{\pm}2.83\%$), which motivates careful protocol matching when
studying predictor variants on smaller subsets: a weak absolute CEM
score on 1k windows is expected and should not be confused with a
failed reproduction of the full recipe.

We adopt \LeWM{}'s official encoder, SIGReg recipe, projectors, and CEM
evaluator unchanged, so our study isolates predictor dynamics rather
than re-tuning the full stack.
Relative to Dreamer-style imagination
\cite{hafner2020dreamer,hafner2023dreamerv3} and TD-MPC-style latent
MPC \cite{hansen2022tdmpc}, \LeWM{} (and thus \method{}) is closer to
the latter in \emph{decision-time} planning, but uses a JEPA + SIGReg
representation objective instead of reconstruction or TD-trained
task-oriented latents.

\subsection{Neural ODEs, Latent ODEs, and continuous-time world models}
\label{sec:rel-ode}

Neural ODEs~\cite{chen2018neuralode} parameterize continuous
transformations via $\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}t = v_\theta(z,t)$ and
backpropagate through black-box ODE solvers (adjoint or
discretize-then-optimize).
This viewpoint influenced continuous-depth residual networks,
continuous normalizing flows, and time-series models that treat
latent evolution as a flow rather than a discrete jump.
Latent ODEs~\cite{rubanova2019latentode} extend the idea to
irregularly sampled sequences: an ODE-RNN recognition network and a
generative Latent ODE define continuous hidden dynamics between
observations, naturally handling non-uniform gaps.

For world modeling, continuous dynamics are attractive because
physical time is continuous and discrete next-step maps must absorb
an entire transition into one nonlinear layer.
\PriorODEWorld{}~\cite{liu2026odeworld} introduces Physical-Time Flow
(PT-Flow): a continuous latent velocity field integrated by an ODE
solver for video and control, emphasizing dynamical structure beyond
discrete next-step prediction.
Under PT-Flow, future prediction becomes temporal integration in a
compressed latent space; enforcing ODE structure on the
representation and velocity field is argued to mitigate representation
collapse and to support arbitrary temporal resolution (including
backward prediction), with applications to generation and robotic
control.

Our \method{} setting is narrower and deliberately comparable.
We keep \LeWM{}'s JEPA loss and planning loop, and redefine a single
discrete planning step as integrate $\tau{:}0\to 1$ under an
action-conditioned velocity field $v_\theta(z,a)$ with classical RK4.
We do \emph{not} claim the full Physical-Time Flow agenda (adaptive
physical time, multi-scale horizons, reconstruction /
generation pathways, or backward prediction); instead we ask whether
the ODE inductive bias alone helps under a matched \LeWM{} protocol.
Relative to Latent ODEs~\cite{rubanova2019latentode}, we do not model
irregular observation times or variational recognition: actions are
piecewise constant on each planning interval, and training uses
uniform expert windows from official PushT HDF5.

\subsection{Discrete AR predictors vs.\ continuous-time latent dynamics}
\label{sec:rel-disc-cont}

Discrete autoregressive predictors map $(z_t,a_t)\mapsto\hat z_{t+1}$
in one shot.
They dominate modern JEPA control stacks
\cite{maes2026lewm,assran2025vjepa2}: one forward pass per step,
native alignment with discrete CEM rollouts, and straightforward
BPTT through a fixed-depth transformer block.
Continuous predictors instead learn $v_\theta$ and obtain
$\hat z_{t+1}$ by numerical integration
\cite{chen2018neuralode,liu2026odeworld}.
This costs more compute per step---RK4 with $N{=}4$ substeps evaluates
the velocity field four times per substep interval, sixteen times over
$\tau{:}0\to1$---but exposes intermediate states along the interval
and encourages residual updates ($z(1)\approx z(0)$ when $v\approx 0$),
especially with a zero-initialized linear velocity head.

Conceptually, the discrete map is a black-box one-step operator;
the ODE view factors the same transition as an integral of a learned
vector field, which can (in principle) share structure across step
sizes if physical time were varied.
In our drop-in design we \emph{fix} the outer step size to one
planning tick ($\tau{:}0\to1$), so we do not yet exploit adaptive
horizons; the comparison isolates whether continuous residual
dynamics improve JEPA fit and CEM under equal data and planner
settings.
Our experiments quantify both sides: train/val loss and CEM on a fixed
split, plus wall-clock CEM cost on the same hardware
(\Cref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{CEM, MPC, and planning with learned world models}
\label{sec:rel-cem}

Model-based RL and planning systems often learn latent dynamics and
optimize action sequences by shooting
\cite{chua2018pets,hafner2019planet}.
Cross-entropy method (CEM) samples candidate action sequences, rolls
out the learned dynamics, ranks by a goal- or reward-conditioned cost,
and refits a sampling distribution around elite samples.
PETS~\cite{chua2018pets} popularized probabilistic ensembles with
trajectory sampling; PlaNet~\cite{hafner2019planet} plans in a learned
latent RSSM from pixels; Dreamer~\cite{hafner2020dreamer} instead
amortizes behavior through latent imagination and analytic value
gradients; TD-MPC~\cite{hansen2022tdmpc} returns to short-horizon
latent MPC with a TD-trained terminal value, often using MPPI/CEM-style
sampling at decision time.

\LeWM{} uses the CEM interface with a latent goal embedding derived
from a future observation at a fixed goal offset---closer to
PlaNet/TD-MPC-style decision-time search than to Dreamer's frozen
actor at inference, but with a JEPA + SIGReg world model rather than
reconstruction or TOLD.
We use the official \LeWM{} CEM evaluator on a fixed validation window
set so discrete and ODE predictors share the same starts and
feasibility filter (\Cref{sec:setup}).
Earlier probes on random full-dataset starts are discarded as
protocol-mismatched; all planning claims in this paper use the
val-99 / 93-feasible protocol tied to \texttt{validate/loss}.
Importantly, a better next-embedding loss need not improve CEM:
planner horizons, elite counts, and goal-offset geometry can dominate
in the low-data regime, which is exactly the honest finding we report
(\Cref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{Positioning summary}
\label{sec:rel-position}

Relative to prior work, \method{} is intentionally conservative:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item vs.\ I-JEPA / V-JEPA / V-JEPA~2:
  we do not scale self-supervised video pretraining; we inherit JEPA
  prediction in embedding space inside a control stack already trained
  from scratch on PushT windows.
\item vs.\ \LeWM{} / LeJEPA (SIGReg):
  same JEPA + SIGReg + CEM stack; only the predictor map changes from
  discrete AR to RK4-integrated $v_\theta$.
\item vs.\ \PriorODEWorld{} / Neural ODEs / Latent ODEs:
  we borrow continuous-velocity prediction, but fix
  $\tau\in[0,1]$ per planning step with piecewise-constant actions,
  and do not adopt Physical-Time Flow, adaptive physical time,
  irregular-time recognition, or that prior work's full training recipe.
\item vs.\ Dreamer / TD-MPC / classical CEM planners:
  planning remains official \LeWM{} CEM; we neither train an
  imagination actor nor a TD terminal value---only the dynamics used
  inside CEM change.
\end{itemize}
This positioning keeps comparisons fair on official PushT windows and
makes negative or tied CEM results interpretable rather than confounded
by simultaneous encoder or planner changes.
In short: \method{} is a \emph{drop-in ODE predictor on the LeWM
stack}, not a new world-model family.
